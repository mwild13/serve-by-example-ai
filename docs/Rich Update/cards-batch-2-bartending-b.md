## 26. Soda Gun Speed

5 cards.

### c1. Know your gun by feel

Learn which button is which on your bar's soda gun until you can pour without looking.

*Why it matters:* Every bar's layout is different. Knowing yours by feel keeps your eyes on the guest and the glass.

From: "Learning the soda gun button layout by feel allows you to pour without looking down." (true); "Knowing which button number maps to which syrup is essential bar knowledge." (true)

### c2. Rinse with water, clean the nozzle

Rinse the gun with clean water, not soda, and clean the nozzle by hand every day.

*Why it matters:* Soda leaves sugar behind. Syrup builds up on the nozzle, grows bacteria and taints the drinks.

From: "The soda gun should be flushed with fresh soda after each use to keep it clean." (false); "The soda gun nozzle cleans itself through normal use during service." (false)

### c3. Run the first pour to waste

When the gun hasn't been used for a while, run the first pour away before you serve a guest.

*Why it matters:* Syrup sits warming in the line between uses, so the first pour comes out warm.

From: "A "warm" first pour from the soda gun is caused by syrup sitting in an unrefrigerated line." (true)

### c4. Know where the spare syrup is

Know where the spare bag-in-box syrups are kept. Changing one only takes a couple of minutes.

*Why it matters:* Running out isn't a disaster if you can find the spare. Hunting for it is what holds up service.

From: "Running out of bag-in-box syrup will shut down bar service for at least 30 minutes." (false)

### c5. Wrong button, new drink

If you hit the wrong button, remake the drink. And never top up a guest's drink without asking.

*Why it matters:* Tonic instead of soda changes the drink completely. An uninvited top-up can water down a drink the guest is sipping slowly.

From: "Pressing the wrong button on a soda gun mid-pour means the drink must be remade entirely." (true); "You can top up a guest's drink with the soda gun without asking first." (false)

## 27. Two-Handed Flow

5 cards.

### c1. Both hands working

Keep both hands busy on the same drink: grab the glass with one while the other works the gun.

*Why it matters:* An idle hand is dead time. Pairing actions is the heart of two-handed service.

From: "Expert bartenders eliminate "dead time" by performing two complementary actions simultaneously." (true); "Grabbing a glass with one hand while hitting the soda gun with the other is two-handed service." (true)

### c2. Move less, not faster

Experts don't rush. They cut out steps and pair up actions on purpose.

*Why it matters:* Rushing and juggling too many jobs causes mistakes. Planned double actions are what make you quick.

From: "Moving faster is the main difference between a novice and an expert bartender." (false); "The most efficient bartenders are the ones who tackle the most tasks at once." (false)

### c3. Prep garnishes first

Cut and set up your garnishes before service starts.

*Why it matters:* Fumbling with a knife in a rush is slow and unsafe. With your mise en place done, it's ready when you need it.

From: "Pre-building garnishes before service begins is a waste of valuable prep time." (false)

### c4. No wasted trips

Never walk anywhere empty-handed, and line up the next glass before the first is finished.

*Why it matters:* A forgotten item means a second trip. Lining up the next glass saves a reach that adds up over hundreds of drinks.

From: "Walking to the same destination twice because you forgot something wastes critical service time." (true); "Positioning the next glass under the gun before the first is finished poured saves real time." (true)

### c5. Practise when it's quiet

Use the same efficient habits in quiet periods, not just in a rush.

*Why it matters:* Habits practised when it's calm become automatic when the pressure hits.

From: "Economy of motion habits only matter during a peak rush period." (false)

## 28. Mid-Shift Reset

5 cards.

### c1. Everything in its place

Have everything set up where it belongs before service starts, and keep it that way.

*Why it matters:* Your setup decides how service goes. A tidy, stocked station shows your team you're in control.

From: "Mise en place means having everything in its correct place before service begins." (true); "A tidy, fully-stocked station signals professionalism to your colleagues and management." (true)

### c2. Restock in the lulls

Restock in the short quiet gaps between waves, not just once at the start of the shift.

*Why it matters:* Stations run down fast. The quiet after a wave is your chance to be ready before the next one arrives.

From: "You should restock your station once, at the start of the shift, and that is sufficient." (false); "A 3-minute reload window is impossible to achieve in a real service environment." (false); "The end of a wave is the wrong time to reset your mise en place." (false)

### c3. Never run out of garnish

Keep garnishes topped up so you never have to search for a lime mid-order.

*Why it matters:* A bartender hunting for garnish in a rush shows the whole room the station wasn't ready.

From: "Running out of garnish mid-rush is a visible service failure caused by station mismanagement." (true)

### c4. Ask for cover

If you need to restock, ask a teammate to cover you for a moment.

*Why it matters:* Quick cover is good teamwork. Struggling on silently until you run out is what breaks service.

From: "Asking a colleague to cover for 90 seconds while you restock is bad teamwork." (false)

### c5. Top up the ice

Check and top up the ice in every quiet moment.

*Why it matters:* Running low on ice mid-rush is one of the most common problems behind a bar, and one of the easiest to avoid.

From: "Ice levels should be checked and topped up during every available quiet moment." (true)

## 32. Wine Opener Mastery

5 cards.

### c1. A clean foil cut

Use the foil cutter on your waiter's friend, and cut below the second lip of the bottle.

*Why it matters:* The built-in cutter gives a clean, even edge, and cutting below the lip hides that edge when you pour.

From: "The foil on a wine bottle should be cut below the second lip for a clean presentation." (true); "The foil cutter on a waiter's friend is primarily decorative." (false)

### c2. Two steps, not one pull

Lift the cork in two steps: rest the first step of the lever on the lip and lift, then the second step and lift again.

*Why it matters:* Pulling in one go is how corks break in half. The two steps give you control.

From: "Pulling a cork out in one fast, smooth motion is the professional technique." (false); "Practicing the two-step lever technique prevents the cork from breaking in half." (true)

### c3. Keep it quiet

Ease the cork out silently, without a pop.

*Why it matters:* It's more professional at the table, and with sparkling it keeps the bubbles in the wine.

From: "The cork should be extracted silently rather than with a loud dramatic pop." (true)

### c4. Hold the bottle still

Keep the bottle steady. Only the corkscrew and the lever move.

*Why it matters:* A steady bottle is how you get a clean, controlled extraction.

From: "You should twist the bottle when extracting the cork rather than the corkscrew." (false)

### c5. Present the cork, every bottle

Present the cork so the guest can check it, and open every bottle properly at the table, whatever the price.

*Why it matters:* A moist cork is a good sign; a dry, crumbly one suggests the bottle was stored badly. Every guest gets the same service.

From: "Presenting the extracted cork to the guest allows them to check for signs of a fault." (true); "A waiter's friend is only necessary for expensive bottles of wine." (false)

## 33. Cellar & Kegs

5 cards.

### c1. Fobbing means a problem

A fobbing tap pours mostly foam. If it doesn't clear, find the cause or tell your manager.

*Why it matters:* Fobbing usually means an empty keg, a gas problem or a warm line. It won't fix itself.

From: "A "fobbing" tap means the keg is producing excessive, undrinkable foam." (true); "A tap that is still fobbing after 5 minutes will clear itself without investigation." (false)

### c2. Disengage, then pour off the air

Lift the coupler handle to disengage it before you change a keg. Afterwards, pour off the air in the line.

*Why it matters:* A coupler under pressure can blow out gas and beer. Air left in the line makes the first pours fob.

From: "You should remove a keg coupler without turning off the gas first." (false); "Air bubbles in the beer line cause the first pours to fob heavily after a keg change." (true)

### c3. No gas, no beer

If the beer stops flowing, check the gas bottle.

*Why it matters:* Gas is what pushes beer up the line. When the bottle runs out, every tap on it stops.

From: "An empty gas bottle makes no noticeable difference to the pour quality." (false)

### c4. Slide, don't lift

A full 50-litre keg weighs more than 50kg. Slide it where you can, and lift it properly when you must.

*Why it matters:* Lifting a keg the wrong way causes serious back and shoulder injuries.

From: "A full 50-litre keg weighs more than 50kg and must be handled with correct manual lifting technique." (true)

### c5. Right coupler, regular cleaning

Use the right coupler for each brand, and keep line cleaning on the schedule your venue and brewer set.

*Why it matters:* The wrong coupler damages the keg valve. Lines are cleaned on schedule, before buildup makes the beer taste off, not after.

From: "Kegs from different beer brands can always share the same coupler type." (false); "Beer lines only need cleaning once the beer starts to taste off." (false)

## 34. Tray & Glass Grip

6 cards.

### c1. Hands off the rim

Never touch the rim or the inside of a glass.

*Why it matters:* That's where the guest drinks from. Fingers there spread bacteria, and guests notice.

From: "You should never touch the rim or inside of a glass when handling or carrying it." (true)

### c2. Don't stack glasses

Never carry glasses stacked inside each other.

*Why it matters:* They lock together, and pulling them apart snaps the rims.

From: "Carrying five glasses stacked inside each other is an efficient and safe technique." (false)

### c3. Use the pinch

Carry several glasses at once with the pinch: each held by the base between two fingers and a thumb.

*Why it matters:* It's steady, keeps your fingers off the rims and saves trips. One glass at a time is too slow on the floor.

From: "The "pinch" technique involves holding a glass by the base with two fingers and a thumb." (true); "Glasses should always be carried individually to prevent breakage." (false)

### c4. Stems for wine

Carry wine glasses by the stem.

*Why it matters:* A hand on the bowl warms chilled wine and leaves smudges the guest can see.

From: "Carrying wine glasses by the stem keeps fingerprints off the bowl and maintains temperature." (true)

### c5. Heavy in the middle, dry before carrying

Put heavy glasses in the centre of a tray, and let washed glasses drain before you carry them.

*Why it matters:* Weight in the centre keeps the tray steady. Wet glass slips out of your grip.

From: "The heaviest glasses should be placed at the outer edge of a tray for easy access." (false); "Wet or freshly washed glasses are significantly more likely to slip from your grip." (true)

### c6. Plates and glasses apart

Never carry plates and glasses in the same hand.

*Why it matters:* Food gets onto the glasses, and the two loads pull against each other.

From: "Plates and glasses can be carried together in the same hand if you are careful." (false)

## 39. Bar-Back Synergy

5 cards.

### c1. Keep the bartender supplied

Your job is to make sure the bartender never runs out. Taking orders is theirs.

*Why it matters:* The bartender can only serve as fast as you keep them stocked. You handle supply so they can focus on guests.

From: "The bar back's primary role is to ensure the bartender never runs out of essential supplies." (true); "The bar back is responsible for taking guest drink orders during a rush." (false)

### c2. Go where you're needed

Cross the floor when the job needs it: ice runs, collecting glasses, restocking.

*Why it matters:* Supply and support don't stop at the end of the bar.

From: "Bar backs must stay behind the bar at all times and never cross the floor." (false)

### c3. Call out, come from behind

Call out before you move behind the bartender, and fill ice from the back of the bar.

*Why it matters:* Moving silently behind someone in a rush risks a collision. Working from the back keeps you out of their way.

From: "Calling out to a bartender before moving ice behind them during service prevents a collision." (true); "Filling ice bins from the back of the bar without disrupting the bartender is a core bar-back skill." (true)

### c4. Don't wait to be asked

Look for the next job yourself, and fill any empty slot on the speed rail straight away.

*Why it matters:* An empty slot makes the bartender stop serving to find a bottle. Your job is to stop that moment happening.

From: "A bar back who finishes their tasks early should stand by and wait to be asked for help." (false); "The speed rail must be restocked by the bar back the moment it shows any empty slots." (true)

### c5. Talk all through the rush

Keep talking through the rush: a nod, a tap, a one-word call.

*Why it matters:* Quick, constant signals keep service flowing. Waiting for a quiet moment is too late.

From: "Bar back and bartender communication should only happen during quiet moments in service." (false)
