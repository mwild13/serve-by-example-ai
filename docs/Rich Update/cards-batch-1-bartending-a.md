## 1. Beer Pouring

6 cards.

### c1. Start with a cold glass

Never pour beer into a glass that's still warm from the glass washer.

*Why it matters:* Heat knocks the gas out of the beer. You get a flat pour with too much foam, and the beer is wasted.

From: "A warm glass from the dishwasher ruins the beer's carbonation." (true)

### c2. Tilt, then open the tap fully

Hold the glass at 45 degrees and open the tap fully in one sharp movement. Bring the glass upright as it fills.

*Why it matters:* The beer runs down the side instead of crashing to the bottom, which keeps the head under control. A half-open tap makes more foam, not less.

From: "You should always hold the glass at a 45-degree angle." (true); "Pouring draught beer slowly prevents excess foam from forming." (false)

### c3. The tap never touches the beer

Keep the nozzle clear of the glass and the beer for the whole pour.

*Why it matters:* A nozzle dipped in beer picks up bacteria and passes it on to every glass after. It also breaks health rules.

From: "You should dip the tap nozzle into the beer." (false)

### c4. A schooner and its head

In most states a schooner holds 425ml. Finish it with a head of 1 to 1.5 cm. South Australia names its glasses differently.

*Why it matters:* The head holds in the gas and the aroma. Much more than that and the guest is short-changed on beer.

From: "A standard schooner glass holds exactly 425ml of liquid." (true); "The ideal head on a schooner is roughly 1.5 cm." (true)

### c5. Don't let it overflow

Stop the pour at the right level. Beer running down the outside of the glass is never normal.

*Why it matters:* It's stock the venue has paid for and can't sell, it hurts the GP, and the guest is left holding a sticky glass.

From: "Overflowing the beer down the sides of the glass is standard." (false)

### c6. Buttery beer means a dirty line

If draught beer tastes like butter, tell your manager: the line needs cleaning.

*Why it matters:* The buttery taste is diacetyl, which builds up in lines that haven't been cleaned. Every pour from that tap tastes off until it's fixed.

From: "A dirty beer line makes the beer taste like butter." (true)

## 2. Wine Service

6 cards.

### c1. Shiraz isn't served fridge-cold

Serve full-bodied reds like Shiraz at about 16 to 18°C, not straight from the fridge.

*Why it matters:* Too cold and the fruit and aroma shut down, so the guest tastes very little of what they paid for.

From: "Australian Shiraz is best served chilled from the fridge." (false)

### c2. Know your pour

A standard glass of wine is usually 150ml, and a 750ml bottle gives five of them.

*Why it matters:* The right pour keeps every glass the same for guests and keeps the bottle count right. A 150ml glass is about 1.4 to 1.6 standard drinks.

From: "A standard glass of wine is usually 150ml in Australia." (true); "A standard wine bottle holds exactly 750ml of liquid." (true)

### c3. Show the label first

Present the bottle with the label facing the guest before you open it.

*Why it matters:* It lets the guest check it's the wine and the vintage they ordered before it's opened.

From: "You must always present the bottle label to the guest." (true)

### c4. Corked means tainted

A corked wine is tainted, not full of cork. It smells like damp cardboard or a wet dog.

*Why it matters:* Bits of cork in the glass are harmless. Cork taint (TCA) spoils the wine, so take a guest seriously when they say it smells musty.

From: "Corked wine means there are pieces of cork in it." (false)

### c5. Open sparkling quietly

Open sparkling wine with a soft hiss, never a loud pop.

*Why it matters:* A quiet opening keeps the bubbles in the wine and keeps the dining room calm. A flying cork can hurt someone.

From: "You should pop a sparkling wine cork loudly for effect." (false)

### c6. Styles and glasses

Marlborough Sauvignon Blanc is dry and crisp. Red wine glasses have larger bowls than white wine glasses.

*Why it matters:* Knowing the style helps you guide a guest. The bigger bowl gives bold reds more air, so they open up and release their aroma.

From: "Sauvignon Blanc from Marlborough is a very dry, crisp white." (true); "Red wine glasses generally have larger bowls than white glasses." (true)

## 3. Cocktail Fundamentals

6 cards.

### c1. Measure the spirit

Most venues pour 30ml as a standard spirit measure. Use the jigger every time.

*Why it matters:* The same measure every time keeps drinks consistent, keeps the venue's stock counts right and keeps service responsible.

From: "A standard spirit pour in an Australian venue is 30ml." (true)

### c2. Stir a Martini

Stir a Martini. Don't shake it.

*Why it matters:* Stirring chills it and keeps it crystal clear. Shaking clouds it with air bubbles and waters it down.

From: "You must shake a Martini to make it perfectly clear." (false)

### c3. Syrup and mint

Simple syrup is equal parts sugar and water. Press mint gently, never grind it.

*Why it matters:* Syrup balances the sour in most classic cocktails. Crushing the mint stems releases bitter flavours that ruin a Mojito.

From: "Simple syrup is made of equal parts sugar and water." (true); "Muddling mint too hard makes a Mojito taste very bitter." (true)

### c4. Fresh coffee for an Espresso Martini

Make an Espresso Martini with a fresh, hot shot of espresso.

*Why it matters:* The oils in fresh espresso are what shake up into the thick crema on top. Old or cold coffee gives a flat drink.

From: "An Espresso Martini requires fresh hot espresso to froth properly." (true)

### c5. Classic specs, right glass

A Negroni is equal parts gin, Campari and sweet vermouth. Every cocktail has its own glass.

*Why it matters:* Guests know the classic specs. Coupes, rocks glasses and highballs each suit a different kind of drink.

From: "A classic Negroni contains gin, Campari, and sweet vermouth." (true); "All cocktails must be served in a tall highball glass." (false)

### c6. Never scoop with a glass

Always use the ice scoop. Never scoop ice with a glass.

*Why it matters:* If the glass chips, you can't see the shards in the ice, and the whole well has to be burnt.

From: "You should scoop ice using the glass to save time." (false)

## 4. Barista Basics

5 cards.

### c1. Don't boil the milk

Stop texturing milk before it reaches 65°C. Never boil it.

*Why it matters:* Above 65°C milk burns and loses its natural sweetness, and the whole coffee tastes scorched.

From: "Milk for a flat white should be boiled past 75°C." (false)

### c2. Shot time and roast

A single espresso shot runs for about 25 to 30 seconds. Darker beans don't make a sweeter shot.

*Why it matters:* Too fast tastes sour and too slow tastes bitter. Dark roasts are more bitter; lighter roasts keep more natural sweetness.

From: "A standard single espresso shot takes about 25 to 30 seconds." (true); "Dark roasted beans always produce a sweeter espresso crema." (false)

### c3. Know the Aussie menu

A macchiato is a shot with a dash of textured milk. A cappuccino has more foam than a flat white.

*Why it matters:* Guests order by name and expect the same drink every time. The milk is what makes it the drink they asked for.

From: "An Australian macchiato is just a shot with a dash of milk." (true); "A cappuccino has more milk foam than a flat white." (true)

### c4. Purge the wand, flush the group head

Purge the steam wand before and after milk, and flush the group head between every shot.

*Why it matters:* Purging clears water before and milk after. Flushing clears old grounds that make the next coffee taste burnt.

From: "You must purge the steam wand before and after texturing milk." (true); "The group head must be flushed between every single coffee extraction." (true)

### c5. Plant milks need a gentler touch

Texture soy and other plant milks more gently, and not as hot as cow's milk.

*Why it matters:* They have less fat and protein than full cream milk, so they split if you treat them the same way.

From: "Soy milk textures exactly the same as full cream cow's milk." (false)

## 5. Tray Carrying

6 cards.

### c1. Eyes up

Look where you're walking, not at the drinks.

*Why it matters:* Watching the glasses throws off your balance. Eyes up lets you move through the guests safely.

From: "You should always look directly at the drinks while walking." (false)

### c2. Heavy glasses in the middle

Put the heaviest glasses, like schooners, in the centre of the tray.

*Why it matters:* Weight in the middle keeps the tray's balance point low and steady.

From: "Heavy schooner glasses should be placed in the tray's centre." (true)

### c3. One hand under, one hand free

Carry the tray on the fingertips of your non-dominant hand. Keep your dominant hand free.

*Why it matters:* Fingertips make small balance corrections. The free hand opens doors, moves chairs and protects the tray from bumps.

From: "Balancing the tray on your fingertips gives the most stability." (true); "You should carry a loaded drinks tray with two hands." (false); "Keep your dominant hand free to open doors or guide people." (true)

### c4. Drinks trays carry drinks

Never clear plates onto a drinks tray.

*Why it matters:* Food scraps make the tray unstable and dirty the clean glasses beside them.

From: "Clearing empty plates onto a drinks tray is highly efficient." (false)

### c5. Never by the rim

Carry stemmed glasses by the stem or the base, never by the rim.

*Why it matters:* Fingers on the rim are unhygienic and leave marks right where the guest drinks.

From: "Carrying three wine glasses by the rim is safe practice." (false)

### c6. Unload evenly

Take glasses off from the outside edges, evenly from both sides.

*Why it matters:* Unload one side only and the weight shifts, and the tray can flip.

From: "Unloading a tray from one side causes it to flip." (true)

## 6. Sanitation Basics

6 cards.

### c1. Wash your hands after cash

Wash your hands after handling cash, before you touch garnishes or clean glasses.

*Why it matters:* Money is one of the dirtiest things in a venue. Washing stops it spreading to food and drinks.

From: "You must wash your hands after handling dirty cash." (true)

### c2. Protect the ice well

Never spray cleaner near the ice well. If spray or broken glass gets into it, melt the whole well.

*Why it matters:* Spray drifts into the ice, and broken glass is invisible in it. Melting the lot with hot water is the only safe fix.

From: "Spraying surface cleaner directly over the ice well is safe." (false); "Broken glass in the ice well means melting the entire well." (true)

### c3. Bathroom gear stays out

Mops and buckets from the bathrooms never come behind the bar.

*Why it matters:* Cleaning gear is colour-coded so bathroom germs stay out of food and drink areas.

From: "Mops used in the bathrooms can be used behind the bar." (false)

### c4. Sign every spill

Put a wet floor sign on a spill straight away.

*Why it matters:* Slips are the most common injury in a venue. Mark the hazard first, then clean it up.

From: "A wet floor sign must be placed over spills immediately." (true)

### c5. Sanitise, don't just rinse

Clean food prep areas with food-grade sanitiser, not just warm water.

*Why it matters:* Warm water lifts the dirt you can see. Only sanitiser kills the bacteria you can't.

From: "Warm water alone is enough to sanitize food prep areas." (false)

### c6. Drip trays and towels

Flush the beer drip trays with hot water every night, and swap damp bar towels often.

*Why it matters:* Old beer turns to sludge that blocks drains and draws fruit flies. Damp, warm towels breed bacteria.

From: "Beer drip trays should be flushed with hot water nightly." (true); "Leaving damp bar towels on the counter breeds heavy bacteria." (true)

## 7. Bar-Back Efficiency

5 cards.

### c1. Cool, unstacked glasses

Let glasses cool before beer goes in them, and never stack them wet.

*Why it matters:* Hot glasses make beer foam over and waste the keg. Wet glasses stacked together seal tight and break.

From: "Glassware must be cooled before serving draught beer in it." (true); "Stacking wet glasses speeds up the drying process." (false)

### c2. Spirits and ice come first

Keep the speed rail stocked and the ice wells full, even in a rush.

*Why it matters:* With no spirits the whole bar stops. A full well stays colder for longer, so drinks aren't watery.

From: "Replacing empty spirit bottles is the lowest priority mid-rush." (false); "Ice wells should always be filled completely to the top." (true)

### c3. Cut the gas before a keg change

Before you take a coupler off an empty keg, cut its gas: lift the coupler handle to disengage it, then twist it off.

*Why it matters:* A coupler taken off under pressure sprays gas and beer. Most cellars run several kegs off one regulator, so lifting the handle is how you cut it.

From: "Changing an empty keg requires cutting the gas to that coupler first." (true)

### c4. Clear the bar, rotate the juice

Clear empty glasses off the bar top, and use the oldest juice first.

*Why it matters:* Clearing keeps the bar turning over. First in, first out stops juice spoiling and cuts waste.

From: "Bar backs are not responsible for clearing the bar top." (false); "You should rotate fresh juices so the oldest is used first." (true)

### c5. Call it out

Tell the bartender what you're doing: "behind you", "more ice coming".

*Why it matters:* A quick call stops them stepping back into you during a rush.

From: "Constant communication with the bartender prevents service delays." (true)
