# Cards batch 6: Compliance, part B

30 Learn cards to review. Each module opens with these before its quiz. A card has a title, one rule, and why it matters. Under each card is the True/False statement it's written from, with that statement's answer.

Nothing here is live until you approve it.

## 21. Call Behind

5 cards.

### c1. Call "Behind!" every time

Call "Behind!" whenever you pass behind someone, with or without a tray.

*Why it matters:* One unannounced pass can cause a dropped tray or a burn. Collisions happen empty-handed too.

From: "Calling "Behind!" before moving past someone in a narrow bar space is required." (true); "You only need to call "Behind!" when carrying a full tray." (false)

### c2. Call "Corner!"

Call "Corner!" before you step around a blind corner.

*Why it matters:* It gives anyone coming the other way a moment to stop.

From: "The word "Corner!" is used to warn others before stepping around a blind corner." (true)

### c3. Never push or run

Don't push past colleagues, and don't run. Walk fast, with purpose.

*Why it matters:* Pushing and running cause drops, spills and injuries, and the busier it is, the worse they get.

From: "It is fine to push past a colleague without warning during a rush." (false); "Staff should run between the bar and kitchen during busy service." (false)

### c4. Coming through

In a crowd, turn sideways, tuck your elbows in and call "Coming through!"

*Why it matters:* You take up less space, and the call tells guests and staff to clear a path.

From: "Compressing your body sideways when moving through a crowd reduces collision risk." (true); ""Coming through!" is the correct call when navigating a crowded dining room." (true)

### c5. Everyone makes the calls

Use the calls however experienced you are.

*Why it matters:* Collisions don't care how long you've worked in hospitality. The habit keeps everyone safe.

From: "Vocal safety commands are only used by inexperienced staff members." (false)

## 22. Ice Well Burn

5 cards.

### c1. Melt it all

If a glass breaks in the ice well, melt the whole well. Never just pick out the pieces you can see.

*Why it matters:* Tiny shards stay hidden in the ice. Only melting all of it is safe.

From: "If a glass breaks in the ice well, you must melt the entire well immediately." (true); "You can scoop out visible glass pieces and continue using the remaining ice." (false)

### c2. Hot water burns the well

Burn the well by pouring hot water over the ice until it has all melted and drained.

*Why it matters:* Hot water melts the ice fastest and flushes any shards down the drain.

From: "Hot water is poured into the well to melt all ice during a Burn Protocol." (true)

### c3. Burn it straight away

Burn the well the moment glass breaks in it, however busy the bar is.

*Why it matters:* Every drink poured from that ice could hurt a guest. It can't wait until close.

From: "A glass breaking in the ice well only needs to be reported, not cleaned immediately." (false); "You can wait until the venue closes before burning a contaminated ice well." (false)

### c4. Scoop, then a clean holder

Never scoop ice with a glass. Put the scoop back in its clean holder after every use.

*Why it matters:* Glass on glass is how wells get contaminated. A scoop left in the ice or on the bar picks up bacteria.

From: "Using a glass to scoop ice is the leading cause of ice well glass contamination." (true); "The ice scoop must be returned to a clean holder after every single use." (true)

### c5. Plan for the time it takes

A burn takes a while, depending on the size of the well. Plan for that well to be out of service.

*Why it matters:* Melting, draining and checking a well properly can't be rushed.

From: "A proper Burn Protocol can be completed in under two minutes." (false)

## 24. Ice Is Food

5 cards.

### c1. Ice is food

Treat ice as food. It's covered by food safety rules.

*Why it matters:* Ice goes straight into drinks, so contaminating it is as serious as contaminating food.

From: "Ice is classified as a food product under Australian food safety regulations." (true)

### c2. Scoop only

Only ever use the ice scoop: never your hands, even gloved, and never a glass.

*Why it matters:* Hands bring warmth and bacteria. Glass chips, and the chips disappear into the ice.

From: "Using bare hands to transfer ice is acceptable if you are wearing food-safe gloves." (false); "You can use a pint glass to scoop ice in an emergency if the scoop is missing." (false)

### c3. Handle up, damaged scoops out

Keep the scoop handle up, out of the ice. Take a cracked or chipped scoop out of service.

*Why it matters:* A buried handle puts your hand over the ice. A broken scoop sheds plastic into drinks.

From: "The ice scoop handle must always be kept pointing upward, out of the ice." (true); "A cracked or chipped ice scoop must be removed from service immediately." (true)

### c4. Lid shut, machine clean

Keep the ice machine lid closed, and clean the machine on its regular schedule.

*Why it matters:* An open lid lets in dust and insects, and bio-film builds up inside a machine that isn't cleaned.

From: "The ice machine lid can be left open during service for faster access." (false); "Ice machines must be cleaned and sanitized on a regular scheduled cycle." (true)

### c5. Moving ice between wells

Move ice between wells only with the scoop and the designated clean ice bucket.

*Why it matters:* Moving ice follows the same hygiene rules as serving it.

From: "Ice from one well can be freely transferred to another well using any clean container." (false)

## 25. Allergy Shield

5 cards.

### c1. Mark the ticket, tell the kitchen

Mark every allergy clearly on the POS ticket, and tell the kitchen in person straight after the order.

*Why it matters:* Tickets can be missed in a rush. Telling the chef directly confirms they know.

From: "A guest's allergy must be verbally relayed to the kitchen immediately after taking the order." (true); "The POS ticket must be clearly marked with all allergy and dietary information." (true)

### c2. Ask with every order

Ask about allergies with every order. A warning on the menu isn't enough.

*Why it matters:* Menu warnings are only a starting point. Any guest at any table can have an allergy.

From: ""Contains nuts" printed on a menu is sufficient and requires no further staff action." (false); "Asking about allergies is only required for large group bookings." (false)

### c3. Traces matter

A severe nut allergy can react to traces left on shared equipment.

*Why it matters:* A shared board or pan is enough to cause anaphylaxis, which can be life-threatening.

From: "A severe nut allergy can trigger anaphylaxis from trace amounts on shared equipment." (true)

### c4. You pass it on

Never ask a guest to tell the kitchen about their own allergy.

*Why it matters:* You're the link between the guest and the kitchen. That job can't be handed to the guest.

From: "If you are busy, the guest can relay their own allergy directly to the kitchen." (false)

### c5. Check before it goes out

Check with the chef before serving a dish that may hold an allergen. Treat every intolerance with the full allergy process.

*Why it matters:* The final check is the last safety net, and you can't tell how sensitive a guest is.

From: "Checking with the chef before serving a suspected allergen dish is mandatory." (true); "A guest who says they are "just a little intolerant" requires no allergy protocol." (false)

## 31. Jigger Precision

5 cards.

### c1. Jigger every pour

Most venues measure a single spirit at 30ml. Use your venue's jigger for every spirit, in cocktails and mixed drinks alike.

*Why it matters:* Measuring every pour keeps drinks consistent and keeps the bar compliant.

From: "The standard single spirit measure in an Australian venue is 30ml." (true); "The jigger is only required for cocktails, not for spirit-and-mixer drinks." (false)

### c2. Small over-pours add up

Never free-pour. 5ml over a drink sounds small, but over 200 drinks that's a litre given away.

*Why it matters:* Even experienced free-pourers drift over, and it comes straight off the bar's gross profit.

From: "Free-pouring spirits is just as accurate as using a jigger if you have experience." (false); "Over-pouring spirits by 5ml per drink is too small an amount to affect the venue's profit." (false)

### c3. Read the meniscus

Fill the jigger to the meniscus, the slight curve at the top, and check it at eye level.

*Why it matters:* From above, the measure is hard to judge. At eye level you get it right.

From: "The meniscus is the slight curve of liquid at the top of a full jigger." (true)

### c4. The jigger backs you up

Measure every pour, so you can show it was right.

*Why it matters:* If a guest says they were short-poured, the jigger backs you up. Consistent pours also keep the stocktake matching sales.

From: "Using a jigger protects the bartender from accusations of short-changing a guest." (true); "Consistent 30ml pours ensure the venue's stocktake aligns with actual sales data." (true)

### c5. No "bit extra"

Don't add a bit extra when a guest asks. Only a manager can approve that.

*Why it matters:* An unapproved over-pour is a compliance problem, and it's giving away the venue's stock.

From: "Guests who ask for "a bit extra" can be accommodated as a goodwill gesture." (false)

## 35. The Clean Close

5 cards.

### c1. Close for the morning crew

Leave the venue clean and stocked, dry store included, so the morning crew can start straight away.

*Why it matters:* A good close is the best thing you can do for the next shift. They should only have to start, not clean up.

From: "A perfect close is described as the greatest gift you can give the morning shift." (true); "Restocking dry goods at close is a task that can be left for the opening shift." (false)

### c2. Backflush the group heads

Backflush and clean the coffee machine's group heads at every close.

*Why it matters:* Coffee oils left overnight go rancid and make the first coffees bitter.

From: "Backflushing the group heads at close is unnecessary if the machine is used again the next morning." (false)

### c3. Speed rail and glassware

Sanitise the speed rail and wipe it dry, and polish and rack all the glassware.

*Why it matters:* Sticky spirits breed bacteria and draw fruit flies. Spotty glasses in the rack embarrass the venue next day.

From: "The speed rail must be sanitized and wiped completely dry every single night." (true); "All glassware must be polished and correctly racked before the close checklist is signed off." (true)

### c4. Floors and mop water

Mop the floor every night, quiet ones included, and empty the mop bucket.

*Why it matters:* Dirty water left overnight breeds bacteria and smells. Quiet nights are the easiest time to mop properly.

From: "Leaving one mop bucket of dirty water overnight saves time for the morning shift." (false); "The floor only needs to be swept, not mopped, on quiet nights." (false)

### c5. Sign the checklist

Complete and sign the closing checklist.

*Why it matters:* It's the record that the venue was left safe and the standard was met.

From: "A signed-off close checklist creates accountability and confirms the venue was left in a safe condition." (true)
## Points to check

**Worth a look:**
- **Jigger Precision c2:** "5ml over 200 drinks is a litre" is the only number in this batch apart from 30ml. It's a simple sum from the statement, not a venue standard.
- **Jigger Precision c1:** 30ml is "most venues", as fixed in Phase 3.
- **Ice Well Burn c5:** "a burn takes a while", with no time given, as fixed in Phase 3.
- **Ice Is Food c4:** the ice machine is cleaned "on its regular schedule", with no frequency given.
- **The Clean Close c4:** "mop the floor every night" comes from the statement. If any of your venues don't mop nightly, I'll change it to "on your venue's schedule".
