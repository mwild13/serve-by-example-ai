## 15. RSA Compliance

5 cards.

### c1. Refuse when intoxicated

You must refuse service to anyone unduly intoxicated. Ordering food doesn't change that, and there's no "one last drink".

*Why it matters:* Serving them risks heavy fines, your RSA and the venue's licence. Service stops the moment the signs show.

From: "You can serve an intoxicated person if they order food." (false); "You must legally refuse service to any unduly intoxicated patron." (true); "A heavily swaying patron is fine for one last beer." (false)

### c2. Know the signs

Slurred speech and loss of coordination are clear signs of intoxication.

*Why it matters:* Spotting the signs early means you can stop service before it becomes a problem.

From: "Slurring words and loss of coordination are clear signs of intoxication." (true)

### c3. Minors and ID

Never serve alcohol to a minor, even if a parent orders it. Accept only government-issued photo ID.

*Why it matters:* Rules vary by state, but venue policy is to refuse. A licence, passport or proof-of-age card counts; a student card doesn't.

From: "Staff can serve a minor alcohol on licensed premises if a parent orders it for them." (false); "You can accept a university ID card as proof of age." (false)

### c4. Free water, always

Free drinking water must always be available to guests.

*Why it matters:* It's an RSA requirement in licensed Australian venues.

From: "Offering free water is a mandatory RSA requirement in Australia." (true)

### c5. Point to the law

When you refuse service, explain that the law requires it. It isn't your personal choice.

*Why it matters:* "I could lose my job and face a big fine" takes the refusal off you personally, which calms things down.

From: "Blaming the law is a great way to de-escalate a refusal." (true)

## 16. Food Safety

6 cards.

### c1. Coeliac means separate gear

For a coeliac guest, use completely separate preparation equipment.

*Why it matters:* Even a crumb of gluten on a shared board can put a coeliac guest in hospital.

From: "A coeliac allergy requires entirely separate preparation equipment." (true)

### c2. Keep raw meat apart

Store raw chicken on the bottom shelf, and never use the same tongs for raw and cooked meat.

*Why it matters:* Raw juices that drip down or ride on tongs spread bacteria like salmonella onto ready-to-eat food.

From: "Chicken can be safely stored on the top fridge shelf." (false); "You can use the same tongs for raw and cooked meat." (false)

### c3. Out of the danger zone

Keep food out of the danger zone, 5°C to 60°C. Thaw in the fridge or under cold running water, never on the bench.

*Why it matters:* Bacteria multiply fast between 5°C and 60°C. Bench thawing leaves the outside of the food there for hours.

From: "Food must be kept out of the temperature danger zone." (true); "It is safe to defrost raw prawns on the bench overnight." (false)

### c4. Twenty seconds

Wash your hands with soap and water for at least 20 seconds.

*Why it matters:* A quick rinse doesn't kill germs. It's the time and the rubbing that do.

From: "Washing your hands takes a minimum of twenty seconds." (true)

### c5. Label and date

Label and date prepped food, so the oldest is used first.

*Why it matters:* First in, first out cuts waste and stops old food being served.

From: "Date labels ensure a strict first-in, first-out stock rotation." (true)

### c6. Blue band-aids

Cover cuts in the kitchen with a blue band-aid.

*Why it matters:* Blue doesn't occur naturally in food, so you'll see it straight away if it falls off.

From: "Blue band-aids should be worn over cuts in the kitchen." (true)

## 17. Conflict De-escalation

5 cards.

### c1. Open body, safe distance

Keep your arms uncrossed and your body relaxed, and keep a safe distance.

*Why it matters:* Crossed arms look defensive, and stepping in close can trigger a fight-or-flight reaction.

From: "Crossing your arms shows authority when dealing with an angry patron." (false); "You should stand aggressively close to an intoxicated angry patron." (false)

### c2. Calm voice, no shouting match

Keep your voice calm and steady. Never argue back loudly.

*Why it matters:* People mirror tone, so calm brings their volume down. A shouting match can turn physical.

From: "Keeping a calm and steady voice helps lower the guest's anger." (true); "Arguing back loudly proves to the crowd that you are right." (false)

### c3. Away from the crowd

Move a dispute away from the main crowd where you can.

*Why it matters:* Without an audience the guest has less reason to show off, and the room stays calm.

From: "Moving a dispute away from the main crowd is good practice." (true)

### c4. Address the behaviour

Talk about the behaviour, not the person. A simple apology often helps.

*Why it matters:* It takes the personal sting out, so the guest has less to fight. "I'm sorry that happened" shows you've heard them.

From: "Addressing the bad behaviour rather than the person diffuses tension." (true); "Offering a simple apology can instantly de-escalate a heated complaint." (true)

### c5. Threats go to security

If anyone makes a threat, call security or a manager straight away.

*Why it matters:* Threats are a hard line. Your safety comes first.

From: "You must notify security or a manager if threats are made." (true)

## 18. Evacuation Protocols

5 cards.

### c1. Know your assembly point

Know where your venue's assembly point is. It's a safe distance from the building, never inside.

*Why it matters:* In an emergency there's no time to work it out, and everyone needs to be clear of the building.

From: "The main emergency assembly point is always located inside the venue." (false)

### c2. Leave everything behind

Tell guests to leave their drinks, and leave your own things. Go when the alarm sounds.

*Why it matters:* Drinks spill on stairs and stop people holding the handrails. Bags can be replaced; people can't.

From: "You must instruct patrons to leave their drinks behind during evacuation." (true); "Gathering your personal belongings is the top priority during a fire." (false)

### c3. Stairs, not lifts. Exits clear

Use the fire stairs, never the lifts, and never block a fire exit with chairs or stock.

*Why it matters:* Lifts can fail or open onto the fire. A blocked exit can cause a deadly crush.

From: "Elevators are the safest way to evacuate a burning building." (false); "Fire exits must never be blocked by chairs or stock boxes." (true)

### c4. Follow the fire warden

The fire warden is in charge until the fire brigade arrives. Check the bathrooms only if it's safe and the warden says so.

*Why it matters:* The warden outranks the manager in an emergency. Bathrooms are where people most often miss the alarm.

From: "The fire warden holds total authority during an emergency evacuation." (true); "You should check the bathrooms quickly to ensure no one remains." (true)

### c5. Walk, don't run

Walk quickly and calmly, and tell guests clearly to do the same.

*Why it matters:* Running spreads panic through a crowd. Calm, clear instructions keep people moving safely.

From: "Running out of the building causes dangerous crowd panic." (true)

## 19. Opening & Closing

6 cards.

### c1. Count the float at opening

Count and check the till float when you open.

*Why it matters:* If it's wrong and you don't check, the difference from the night before lands on you.

From: "The till float must be counted and checked at opening." (true)

### c2. The coffee machine is the venue's call

Whether the coffee machine stays on overnight is your venue's rule. Follow it every night.

*Why it matters:* Some machines are built to stay on and some venues switch them off. It's never a personal choice.

From: "Whether the coffee machine stays on overnight is up to each staff member." (false)

### c3. Wipe the taps, dry the wells

Every night, wipe and plug the beer taps, melt the ice and wipe the wells dry.

*Why it matters:* Sugary residue draws fruit flies, and standing water breeds bacteria overnight.

From: "Wiping down beer taps prevents fruit flies from breeding overnight." (true); "All ice must be melted and the wells wiped dry nightly." (true)

### c4. Mop so it dries

Mop at close or early in the morning, never just before the doors open.

*Why it matters:* A wet floor as guests walk in is a slip hazard.

From: "Mopping the floors is best done right before the doors open." (false)

### c5. Lock the safe, set the alarm

Lock and secure the safe every night, and set the alarm as the last step.

*Why it matters:* An unlocked safe is a serious breach. The alarm secures the building once everything else is done.

From: "You should leave the safe unlocked for the morning manager." (false); "Setting the security alarm is the final step of closing." (true)

### c6. Bottle bins in the allowed hours

Empty the bottle bins only in the hours your venue's licence or council rules allow.

*Why it matters:* Tipping glass late at night is loud, and many venues have rules about it.

From: "Emptying the glass bins at 2 AM is quiet and respectful." (false)

## 20. Inventory Control

5 cards.

### c1. Measure every pour

Use a jigger for every spirit. Free-pouring is against most venues' policy.

*Why it matters:* A few extra millilitres a drink adds up fast and eats into the bar's gross profit.

From: "Over-pouring spirits costs the venue thousands of dollars in lost GP." (true); "Using a jigger ensures exact measurements and perfect stock control." (true)

### c2. Free drinks need approval

Never give drinks away. Only a manager can approve a free drink, and it's logged in the POS.

*Why it matters:* Unapproved free drinks are treated as theft of the venue's stock.

From: "Giving free drinks to friends is totally fine in Australian pubs." (false)

### c3. Log waste, count everything

Record every dropped bottle in the wastage log. A stocktake counts every item, open bottles included.

*Why it matters:* The log explains where stock went, so the stocktake balances.

From: "Recording a dropped bottle in the wastage log is mandatory." (true); "Accurate stocktakes depend on counting every single item in the venue." (true)

### c4. First in, first out

Use the oldest stock first, from milk and juice to kegs. Throwing out half-full kegs is never routine.

*Why it matters:* FIFO stops stock expiring. Binning a half keg is a big loss and points to poor stock control.

From: "First-in, first-out means using the oldest stock before the newest." (true); "Throwing away half-full kegs is a standard weekly procedure." (false)

### c5. Never pour it back

Never pour leftover wine from a glass back into the bottle.

*Why it matters:* Once a drink has been in a guest's glass, it goes down the drain. Pouring it back is a serious health breach.

From: "You can pour leftover wine back into the bottle to save money." (false)
