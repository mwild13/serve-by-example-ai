# Cards batch 3: Service, part A

40 Learn cards to review. Each module opens with these before its quiz. A card has a title, one rule, and why it matters. Under each card is the True/False statement it's written from, with that statement's answer.

Nothing here is live until you approve it.

## 8. The Greeting

6 cards.

### c1. Acknowledge straight away

Acknowledge every new guest straight away. In a rush, a nod and a smile count.

*Why it matters:* A quick acknowledgement tells guests they've been seen, and it makes the wait feel shorter.

From: "You must acknowledge a new guest within three seconds." (true); "A friendly nod counts as a valid greeting during a rush." (true)

### c2. Eyes up while you pour

Lift your eyes to new guests, even while you're pouring.

*Why it matters:* You can hold a pour and still make eye contact. It shows you're in control of the bar.

From: "Eye contact is not necessary if you are pouring drinks." (false)

### c3. Don't shout across the room

Never yell a greeting across a busy room. Move closer, or use a wave or a nod.

*Why it matters:* Shouting interrupts every other guest's meal or conversation.

From: "You should yell a greeting across a crowded dining room." (false)

### c4. Smile, and say you'll be right there

Greet guests with a smile. If you're busy, say "I'll be right with you."

*Why it matters:* A smile sets a friendly tone, and the line tells guests they're next, which takes the stress out of the wait.

From: "Smiling when greeting guests sets a positive tone for service." (true); "Saying 'I will be right with you' is highly professional." (true)

### c5. Never look past waiting guests

Don't ignore waiting guests to look busy.

*Why it matters:* Ignored guests feel invisible, and a short wait turns into a complaint.

From: "Ignoring waiting guests makes them think you are working hard." (false)

### c6. A real welcome builds regulars

Give every guest a genuine welcome, especially the locals.

*Why it matters:* Pubs run on familiar faces. A warm greeting is the first step to a regular.

From: "A genuine welcome encourages locals to become regular patrons." (true)

## 9. Table Dynamics

6 cards.

### c1. Clear when everyone's finished

Don't clear plates while anyone at the table is still eating.

*Why it matters:* Clearing half the table makes slower eaters feel rushed and breaks up the meal.

From: "You should clear entree plates while some guests are still eating." (false)

### c2. From the right, never empty-handed

Clear plates from the guest's right where the table allows, and take empty glasses every time you visit.

*Why it matters:* Clearing from the right keeps you out of the guest's way. Taking the empties on every visit saves trips.

From: "Clearing plates from the guest's right is standard practice." (true); "You must clear all empty glasses every time you visit the table." (true)

### c3. Let the table set the pace

Don't drop the bill before it's asked for, and never rush a table through the meal.

*Why it matters:* An early bill tells guests to leave. Guests notice when they're rushed, and they don't come back.

From: "Dropping the bill before the guests ask is excellent service." (false); "Rushing a table through their meal turns tables much faster." (false)

### c4. Check back after the first bites

Check back shortly after the food arrives, once guests have started eating.

*Why it matters:* That's when an undercooked steak or a missing sauce shows up, while it's still easy to fix.

From: "Checking back two minutes after food arrives ensures quality." (true)

### c5. Talk to the whole table

Make eye contact with everyone at the table, not just the loudest person.

*Why it matters:* Including everyone makes each guest feel valued.

From: "Engaging with every guest at the table builds better rapport." (true)

### c6. Kids' meals first

Serve children their meals first.

*Why it matters:* Hungry kids make stressed parents. Feeding them first settles the whole table.

From: "Serving children their meals first keeps the entire table happy." (true)

## 10. Anticipatory Service

6 cards.

### c1. Refill before they ask

Offer the next drink before the first is empty, and top up water without being asked.

*Why it matters:* Guests never go without, and good service feels effortless.

From: "Offering a second drink before the first is empty is good." (true); "You should top up water glasses without being asked." (true)

### c2. Watch without hovering

Keep an eye on every table, so no guest has to flag you down.

*Why it matters:* If a guest has to wave you over, you've missed the moment. Giving privacy means not hovering, not disappearing.

From: "You should always wait for a guest to flag you down." (false); "Leaving a table alone completely ensures they enjoy their privacy." (false)

### c3. Solve it before it's a problem

Bring extra napkins or wipes with messy food like wings or ribs.

*Why it matters:* You fix the mess before the guest has to ask.

From: "Bringing extra napkins with messy finger food is anticipatory service." (true)

### c4. Anticipate needs, not orders

Anticipate what guests will need, like water, cutlery or clearing. Don't decide their order for them.

*Why it matters:* Anticipating needs is care. Guessing what they want to eat is pushy.

From: "Anticipatory service means guessing what the guest wants to eat." (false)

### c5. Remember your regulars

Learn your regulars and what they usually order.

*Why it matters:* "The usual?" makes a guest feel like a VIP and keeps them coming back.

From: "Recognising a regular guest and remembering their order builds loyalty." (true)

### c6. Dessert menu as plates clear

Offer the dessert menu as you clear the mains.

*Why it matters:* That's when guests decide on dessert or coffee, before they settle on being too full.

From: "Offering a dessert menu as plates are cleared increases sales." (true)

## 11. Guest Complaints

5 cards.

### c1. Listen, then apologise

Let the guest finish without interrupting, then apologise and acknowledge their frustration.

*Why it matters:* Most upset guests want to be heard first. Knowing you're on their side takes the heat out.

From: "You should instantly apologise and validate the guest's frustration." (true); "Listening actively without interrupting diffuses most angry guest situations." (true)

### c2. Own it, don't argue

Never blame the kitchen and never argue. Take ownership as a team.

*Why it matters:* Blame makes the venue look disorganised. Win the argument and you still lose the guest.

From: "Blaming the kitchen calms the guest down much faster." (false); "Arguing with a complaining guest proves that you are right." (false)

### c3. Fix it properly

A cold meal goes back to the kitchen to be cooked again, never microwaved. If your venue allows it, offer a drink while they wait.

*Why it matters:* Microwaving ruins the food. A drink gives the guest something while the new meal is made.

From: "Offering a free drink is a good initial service recovery step." (true); "A cold parmy should be microwaved and given straight back." (false)

### c4. Follow up

Check back after the fix to make sure it's right.

*Why it matters:* Following up shows you care, and it closes the loop.

From: "Following up after fixing the issue ensures total guest satisfaction." (true)

### c5. Tell the manager

Tell your manager about every serious food complaint.

*Why it matters:* Managers need to see patterns so they can fix them with the head chef.

From: "You must tell the manager about all serious food complaints." (true)

## 12. Suggestive Selling

5 cards.

### c1. Suggest a step up

Suggest a better option: a premium local gin instead of the house pour, or a pint instead of a schooner.

*Why it matters:* Moving a guest to a better product is up-selling. It's easy when it's a genuine suggestion.

From: "Suggesting a premium local gin instead of house pour is up-selling." (true); "Offering a larger size beer is an easy up-sell technique." (true)

### c2. Suggest what goes with it

Suggest a side with a main, or a wine that suits the dish.

*Why it matters:* Pairing things that go together makes the meal better and lifts the table's spend.

From: "Asking if they want fries with their steak is suggestive selling." (true); "You should never suggest a wine pairing for a meal." (false)

### c3. Help, don't push

Up-sell when it improves the guest's experience. Never push the most expensive item.

*Why it matters:* A good suggestion gives the guest something they enjoy more. Hard selling destroys trust.

From: "Up-selling means forcing the guest to buy the most expensive item." (false); "Up-selling increases venue revenue when it improves the guest's experience." (true)

### c4. Drinks while they read the menu

Suggest a drink, like a cocktail, while guests are still reading the menu.

*Why it matters:* Drinks on the table early keep guests happy while they decide.

From: "Suggesting a cocktail while they read the menu wastes time." (false)

### c5. Know the menu

Know what's on the menu and what it tastes like.

*Why it matters:* You can't recommend a craft beer you've never tried. Knowing the product makes suggestions feel natural.

From: "Knowing the menu thoroughly makes up-selling feel natural." (true)

## 13. VIP Management

6 cards.

### c1. The law applies to everyone

No guest can stay or drink past the venue's licensed trading hours, VIP or not.

*Why it matters:* Licensing laws apply to everyone. Breaking them risks a fine from the liquor regulator and the venue's licence.

From: "VIP guests can legally stay past the venue's licensed trading hours." (false)

### c2. A short grace period

Hold a reserved table for your venue's grace period before you give it away.

*Why it matters:* It allows for traffic and parking delays, without the table sitting empty all night.

From: "Holding a reserved table for a short grace period before giving it away is standard practice." (true)

### c3. Every guest counts

Never move seated guests for a VIP, and never ignore walk-ins because VIPs are in. Offer the VIP a drink at the bar.

*Why it matters:* Moving seated guests causes a scene. Every guest is owed the same basic hospitality.

From: "You should kick regular diners out to seat a walk-in VIP." (false); "You can ignore walk-ins if you have VIPs in the venue." (false)

### c4. Use their name

Remember a VIP's name, and use it.

*Why it matters:* Personal recognition is what makes VIP guests feel valued.

From: "Remembering a VIP's name provides a premium hospitality experience." (true)

### c5. Don't double-seat a section

Don't seat several tables in one section at the same time.

*Why it matters:* It sends the kitchen a flood of dockets at once and swamps the staff.

From: "Double-seating a section overwhelms the staff and ruins service." (true)

### c6. Look after people at the door

Offer water to guests waiting for a table. When you're full, turn people away politely, with a suggestion.

*Why it matters:* Service starts before guests sit down. A friendly no makes them want to come back.

From: "Offering complimentary water while guests wait for tables is expected." (true); "A fully booked venue should still manage the door politely." (true)

## 14. Phone Etiquette

6 cards.

### c1. Answer promptly, name the venue

Answer the venue phone promptly, and say the venue's name.

*Why it matters:* It confirms they've called the right place, and a ringing phone annoys guests near the host stand.

From: "You should answer the venue phone within three rings." (true); "You should always state the venue name when answering." (true)

### c2. Warn before you hang up

If a caller is abusive, warn them politely that you'll end the call, then hang up if it continues.

*Why it matters:* You stay professional even when the caller doesn't.

From: "Hanging up on a rude caller without warning is acceptable." (false)

### c3. Get the details right

Take a name and number for the waitlist, and read booking details back to the caller.

*Why it matters:* Without a number you can't call them when a table frees up. Reading back the date, time and numbers prevents mix-ups.

From: "Taking a name and number is essential for a waitlist." (true); "You must read back the booking details to confirm accuracy." (true)

### c4. Book the time, not the table

Note a request for a particular table, but don't promise it on a busy night.

*Why it matters:* Table plans change all night. Guarantee the booking, not the booth.

From: "Promising a specific booth is risky on a busy Saturday night." (true)

### c5. Don't leave callers waiting

If you're too busy to talk, take their name and number and call them back.

*Why it matters:* A long hold loses the booking. A call back keeps it.

From: "You should put callers on hold for over five minutes." (false)

### c6. Smile on the phone

Smile while you talk on the phone.

*Why it matters:* Smiling changes the shape of your mouth, and the caller can hear the warmth in your voice.

From: "Smiling while speaking makes your tone sound much warmer." (true)
