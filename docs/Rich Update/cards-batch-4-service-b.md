## 23. The Swivel Head

5 cards.

### c1. Spot the dead soldiers

An empty glass left on a table is a "dead soldier". Learn to spot them from across the room.

*Why it matters:* It's one of the clearest signs a table needs you.

From: "An empty glass left on a table is known in the industry as a "dead soldier."" (true)

### c2. No tunnel vision

Keep scanning the room while you work on the task in front of you.

*Why it matters:* Tunnel vision is a sign of being overwhelmed. Experienced staff are always reading the whole room.

From: "Staff should focus exclusively on the task directly in front of them." (false); "Tunnel vision on your immediate task is the sign of an experienced server." (false)

### c3. Read the cues

A guest looking around with a menu open needs you. A raised hand needs you now.

*Why it matters:* Both mean the guest is waiting. A raised hand means their patience is running out, so acknowledge it straight away.

From: "A guest looking around the room while holding a menu likely needs a server." (true); "A raised hand from a guest can wait until you have finished your current task." (false)

### c4. Sweep and acknowledge

Scan the room regularly. When you can't get there yet, catch their eye and nod.

*Why it matters:* Regular sweeps catch needs early. A nod from across the room tells the guest they've been seen.

From: "Scanning the room regularly helps prevent guests from feeling ignored." (true); "Eye contact and a nod from across the room are enough to reassure a waiting guest." (true)

### c5. Everyone reads the room

Watching for empty glasses and guest needs is everyone's job, not just the manager's.

*Why it matters:* A manager can't be everywhere. Everyone on the floor shares the room.

From: "Noticing empty glasses and guest needs is the floor manager's job, not the server's." (false)

## 29. Docket Reading

5 cards.

### c1. Know your docket codes

Learn your venue's docket codes. M/R commonly means medium rare, and G/F gluten free.

*Why it matters:* Systems differ. Misreading a code means the wrong steak, or a missed allergy.

From: ""M/R" on a POS docket means the steak must be cooked to Medium Rare." (true); ""G/F" on a docket means the dish is served with a gravy-free sauce." (false)

### c2. No note isn't a guarantee

No allergy note doesn't mean no allergy. Check any handwritten note you can't read with the server.

*Why it matters:* Guests forget to mention allergies, and handwritten notes often carry them. If you're unsure, ask.

From: "A docket with no special instructions confirms the table has no allergies." (false); "Illegible handwritten additions to a docket can be ignored if they seem minor." (false)

### c3. Keep dockets in order

Work dockets in the order they printed. Only a manager changes that order.

*Why it matters:* The printer queue is the order guests ordered in. Skipping ahead makes tables wait unfairly and sends dishes to the wrong table.

From: "Food tickets are prioritised by the time they are printed, not by table number." (true); "Runners can reorganise dockets to make carrying plates easier when food is ready." (false)

### c4. 86 means it's gone

"86" or "OFF" means the item has run out. Tell the floor staff straight away.

*Why it matters:* Every server needs to know before they take another order for it.

From: ""86" or "OFF" on a kitchen note means that menu item is no longer available." (true)

### c5. Call the table at the pass

Call the table number when food goes up on the pass: "Table 7 away!"

*Why it matters:* The runner collects it straight away, so the food reaches the guest hot.

From: "Calling out a table number when placing food in the pass prevents it from sitting cold." (true)

## 30. Beating the Weed

5 cards.

### c1. Everyone gets in the weed

"In the weed" means completely overwhelmed in a rush. It happens to everyone.

*Why it matters:* What matters is knowing how to work your way out, step by step.

From: ""Being in the weed" is the hospitality term for being completely overwhelmed during a rush." (true)

### c2. Slow down and keep talking

Don't speed up and go quiet. Acknowledge every waiting guest: "With you in a moment."

*Why it matters:* Rushing causes mistakes, and silence makes guests angrier. Being acknowledged resets their patience.

From: "The best way to escape being in the weed is to speed up and stop communicating." (false); "Verbally acknowledging every waiting guest with "With you in a moment" prevents escalation." (true)

### c3. Pause, then work in order

Take a short pause to pick the two or three most important jobs, then work the tickets in the order they came in.

*Why it matters:* Slow is smooth, and smooth is fast. Working in order beats scrambling.

From: "Working tickets in the order they were received prevents reactive chaos during a rush." (true); "Taking a deliberate 30-second pause to assess priorities during a rush is a waste of time." (false)

### c4. Call for help early

Don't take on extra tables when you're overwhelmed. Ask a teammate for help early.

*Why it matters:* An overloaded section makes every table suffer. Calling for backup early is good self-awareness.

From: "Taking on extra tables when you are already overwhelmed shows a strong work ethic." (false); "Asking a colleague for help when you are overwhelmed is a sign of good self-awareness." (true)

### c5. Keep the stress off the floor

Stay calm in front of guests, even when you're flat out.

*Why it matters:* Visible stress makes guests uncomfortable and less likely to come back.

From: "Guests are always understanding when a staff member is visibly struggling." (false)

## 36. Two-Minute Check

5 cards.

### c1. After the first bite

The two-minute check: go back to the table once guests have had their first bite.

*Why it matters:* Before the first bite they can't tell you anything. Soon after, there's still time for the kitchen to fix it.

From: "The two-minute check means returning to a table approximately two minutes after food is served." (true); "The two-minute check must happen after the guest has taken their first bite." (true)

### c2. Every table, every dish

Check every table, whether the order was simple or complicated, new or familiar.

*Why it matters:* Any dish can come out wrong, even the one the kitchen makes every night.

From: "Checking back on a table after food is delivered is only necessary for complicated orders." (false); "The two-minute check is only required for new or unfamiliar menu items." (false)

### c3. Ask a specific question

Ask something specific, like "How's the steak cooked for you?", not "Is everything okay?"

*Why it matters:* "Okay?" gets a one-word answer. A specific question gets a useful one.

From: "Asking "Is everything okay?" is the gold-standard two-minute check question." (false)

### c4. Catch it while it can be fixed

Catch problems early, while a cold or wrong dish can still be cooked again.

*Why it matters:* Once a guest has eaten half a cold meal, the experience can't be fully saved.

From: "A cold steak discovered at the two-minute check can still be refired and saved." (true); "Missing the two-minute window means problems are discovered only when it is too late to fix them." (true)

### c5. A quick check is good service

Keep the check brief and professional. It's part of the meal, not an interruption.

*Why it matters:* It's neglect, not a quick check, that ruins a meal.

From: "A two-minute check interrupts the guest's dining experience and should be kept to a minimum." (false)

## 37. The Out-of-Stock Pivot

5 cards.

### c1. Always offer an alternative

When an item's out, say so, then offer a specific alternative straight away.

*Why it matters:* "Sorry, we're out" is a dead end. The alternative is what saves the sale.

From: "When an item is unavailable, you must always immediately suggest a specific alternative." (true); "Saying "Sorry, we are out of that" and moving on is professional handling of a stock-out." (false); "The pivot technique means acknowledging unavailability and immediately offering a comparable item." (true)

### c2. Recommend, don't console

Offer the closest match, not the cheapest, and present it as a recommendation.

*Why it matters:* "Our house red is very similar and pairs better with that dish" keeps the guest keen instead of disappointed.

From: "Guests always prefer the cheapest alternative when their first choice is unavailable." (false); ""Our house red is very similar and actually pairs better with that dish" is an ideal pivot." (true)

### c3. Know what's similar

Know the menu well enough to know what's similar to what.

*Why it matters:* You can't suggest a real alternative if you don't know what's comparable.

From: "Deep menu knowledge is what allows you to suggest genuine alternatives with confidence." (true)

### c4. One apology is enough

Apologise once, briefly, then move on to the alternative.

*Why it matters:* Over-apologising draws attention to the problem and makes the guest feel worse.

From: "Apologising excessively when an item is unavailable is the mark of good service." (false)

### c5. Everyone knows what's 86'd

All floor staff learn what's 86'd at the start of every service.

*Why it matters:* It stops anyone promising a guest something the kitchen can't make.

From: "Only bartenders need to know about out-of-stock items — the floor staff can improvise." (false)

## 38. Clearing Dead Soldiers

4 cards.

### c1. Clear dead soldiers

An empty glass or bottle on a table is a dead soldier. Clear it without asking.

*Why it matters:* Clearing finished glasses is part of normal service, and it doesn't need the guest's permission.

From: "A "dead soldier" is an empty bottle or glass taking up space on a guest's table." (true); "Removing a dead soldier from a table without first asking the guest is rude." (false)

### c2. Pre-bus as you go

Clear each finished glass and side plate as soon as it's done, not when the whole table has finished.

*Why it matters:* Pre-bussing is standard service. The table stays clean all the way through.

From: "You should only clear a table once the guest has completely finished their meal." (false); "Dead soldiers should only be removed when the entire table has finished drinking." (false)

### c3. Never walk back empty-handed

Every trip to the back clears something. Carry a full load of glasses in one pass.

*Why it matters:* Fewer trips means more time on the floor.

From: "Never walking back to the kitchen empty-handed is a core floor efficiency rule." (true); "Carrying a full arm of cleared glassware to the glass wash in one trip is expert pre-bussing." (true)

### c4. A clean table feels cared for

Keep tables cleared and reset. It doesn't tell guests to leave.

*Why it matters:* A clutter of empties feels like neglect. Only an early bill or hovering pressures guests to go.

From: "A cleared, reset table creates a psychologically cleaner environment for the guest." (true); "Pre-bussing tables signals to guests that they are being asked to leave." (false)

## 40. Natural Upselling

5 cards.

### c1. Name the product

Name what you're suggesting: "With Hendrick's?" or "A pint of that?", not "Premium?" or "A large?"

*Why it matters:* A named product paints a picture. A generic question sounds like a transaction and gets a no.

From: "Naming the product means suggesting a specific premium brand rather than asking a generic question." (true); "Asking "Would you like a large?" is the gold standard for upselling a beer." (false)

### c2. Recommend a specific side

Suggest a particular side: "The sweet potato fries are great with the chicken."

*Why it matters:* It saves the guest deciding. "Any sides?" invites a flat no.

From: "Recommending a specific complementary side dish is more effective than asking "Do you want any sides?"" (true)

### c3. Enthusiasm, not pressure

Suggest things you genuinely like, with real enthusiasm. Never push.

*Why it matters:* Enthusiasm makes it a recommendation. The moment it feels pushy, trust is gone.

From: "Natural upselling is about extracting maximum spend from every guest regardless of their experience." (false); "Genuine enthusiasm when suggesting a premium spirit makes the upsell feel like a recommendation, not a pitch." (true)

### c4. Know your pairings

Learn which wines go with which dishes.

*Why it matters:* A confident pairing sounds like expert advice, not a sales pitch.

From: "Knowing wine pairings transforms every upsell from a sales pitch into an expert recommendation." (true)

### c5. Right time, every guest

Offer dessert as the plates are cleared, and make suggestions to every guest, not just big spenders.

*Why it matters:* Once they've asked for the bill it's too late. Casual guests often welcome a confident suggestion.

From: "The best time to suggest a dessert is after the guest has already asked for the bill." (false); "Upselling techniques only work on guests who are already planning to spend a lot." (false)
